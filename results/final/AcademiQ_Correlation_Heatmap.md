## 1. Academic Understanding: Bivariate Correlation and Multicollinearity

### The Role of Pearson Correlation in Regression Modeling
Pearson's correlation coefficient ($r$) quantifies the direction and strength of linear bivariate relationships:
$$r_{xy} = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})(y_i - \bar{y})}{\sqrt{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 \sum (y_i - \bar{y})^2}}$$

In continuous regression modeling, correlation matrices serve two diagnostic functions:
1. **Target Correlation ($r_{x, y}$):** Identifies features with strong linear associations with `Exam_Score`. Higher absolute values indicate higher individual predictive capability.
2. **Inter-Feature Multicollinearity ($r_{x_j, x_k}$):** Detects redundant or highly collinear predictors. High correlation between independent variables ($|r| > 0.8$) can inflate standard errors in Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression coefficients, causing numerical instability.

## 3. In-Project Significance & Viva Defense

### Why This Heatmap Matters
The correlation matrix provides empirical justification for multiple regression modeling. It proves that while `Hours_Studied` has a solid correlation ($r = 0.44$), relying solely on it omits `Attendance` ($r = 0.58$) and other key drivers. Simultaneously, the lack of high inter-feature collinearity ensures that Multiple Linear Regression coefficients remain well-conditioned, stable, and interpretable.

### Viva Defense Insight
- **Question:** *"Why did Simple Linear Regression achieve only an $R^2$ of $0.23$ when Hours_Studied was used as the single predictor?"*
- **Defense Answer:** In univariate regression, the explained variance equals the squared correlation coefficient: $r^2 \approx 0.44^2 \approx 0.194$ (on training data), reaching $0.23$ on the holdout test set. Over $76\%$ of the score variance is governed by factors outside study hours alone—most notably attendance and contextual resources—demanding a multivariate model.
